\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

An upper confidence bound (UCB) policy adds an uncertainty bonus to a predicted
reward. For a nonlinear predictor the bonus is usually built from gradients,
and retraining changes the gradients of past observations without collecting
new ones. The usual sequential confidence argument needs each regressor to be
fixed before its reward is observed. Gradients recomputed at the current
parameter can depend on the rewards used for training, so collection-time
confidence does not by itself justify a score built from current curvature.
For example, in the scaled-tanh model
$\mu_\theta(z)=\sqrt W\tanh(\phi(z)^\top\theta/\sqrt W)$, the gradient at a
past observation $z_s$ is
$\operatorname{sech}^2(\phi(z_s)^\top\theta/\sqrt W)\,\phi(z_s)$, so its
weight depends on the parameter at which it is evaluated. Recomputed at the
current parameter $\theta_t$, which was fitted to every reward before round
$t$, that weight depends on the reward of round $s$ and on later rewards. The
rebuilt matrix is therefore not a sum of outer products of regressors fixed
before their rewards, which is what the self-normalized confidence argument
requires.

Curvature evaluated at a current trained parameter appears in several
uncertainty methods. Laplace approximations use local inverse curvature
around a MAP solution
\citep{mackay1992practical,kristiadi2020being,daxberger2021laplace},
linearizations of a trained network use implicit Jacobian or Fisher products
with conjugate gradients \citep{maddox2021fast}, and Hessian--vector, GGN and
inverse-curvature products supply the primitives for such solves
\citep{pearlmutter1994fast,schraudolph2002fast,koh2017understanding}.
NeuralUCB and NeuralTS instead keep the gradient features computed when each
observation arrived \citep{zhou2020neural,zhang2021neural}, and fixed NTK
references and learned last-layer uncertainty offer other constructions
\citep{kassraie2022neural,riquelme2018deep}.

This paper first builds a confidence band from collection-time gradients and
then certifies its conversion to the current metric. The band's width, the
\emph{reference width}, is computed from the matrix of gradients stored when
each observation was collected. Its center, the \emph{corrected center}, is
the current prediction plus a correction from a ridge regression on those
stored gradients. \emph{Transport} converts the band into a score that uses
widths in the current metric, multiplied by a factor that a certificate
bounds. On a fixed history, with the same center, confidence multiplier and
error allowance, transport cannot certify a width narrower than the reference
width while it preserves the full reference band; this comparison does not
rank adaptive policies, whose histories differ.

We analyze current-curvature scoring to characterize when it is valid and what
it costs. The paper does not establish a computational advantage for it and
does not propose a better bandit algorithm. Two synthetic studies then ask how
conservative the certificates are and what happens to regret.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Can confidence survive retraining without optimizer convergence?}
For squared loss, a frozen ridge correction gives a confidence band around the
corrected center along any predictable parameter path in a certified region,
given Taylor-remainder and misspecification bounds
(\Cref{cor:corrected-center-new}).
\item \emph{What does conversion to the current metric require, and can it
narrow the width?} A transport theorem combines the reference band with metric,
operator and solver certificates into a UCB score and a regret bound
(\Cref{thm:transported-ucb}). On a fixed history, certified transported widths
cannot fall below the reference width, and an exact actionwise correction
recovers it (\Cref{prop:reference-width-floor}); the symmetric exponential
width factors are sharp (Section~\ref{sec:logarithmic-transport}).
\item \emph{Can the conversion be tight for a model with fixed gradient
directions?} For scaled tanh, termwise comparisons of the gradient weights give
endpoint certificates on a bounded parameter region
(\Cref{cor:tanh-relative-weights,cor:tanh-lipschitz-endpoint}); when $W\ge cT$
the uniform endpoint factor is $1+O(1/(cT))$, so the endpoint-transported bound
has the frozen bound's form (\Cref{cor:tanh-endpoint-regime}).
\item \emph{What happens to regret?} In both synthetic studies,
Hessian-path transport has higher mean cumulative pseudo-regret than the
frozen-reference policy. In a near-linear design cell where the
frozen-reference policy's regret bound is below the deterministic cumulative
pseudo-regret cap, endpoint transport and the frozen-reference policy have
equivalent mean cumulative pseudo-regret within a margin fixed before
evaluation. In more curved cells, removing the Taylor-error allowances lowers
mean regret far more than switching among the reference, endpoint and naive
current widths does (Section~\ref{sec:transport-followup}).
\end{itemize}
Sections~\ref{sec:abstract-transport}--\ref{sec:closed-rates-new} give the
framework, the matrix comparisons, the corrected center and the rates;
Section~\ref{sec:transport-followup} reports the studies, and the proofs are in
\Cref{app:transport-proofs}.
