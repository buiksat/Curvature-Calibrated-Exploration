\section{What confidence transport did in the nonlinear study}
\label{sec:transport-followup}

Does a valid way to transport confidence also give useful decisions and
informative regret bounds?  We study this question in a controlled nonlinear
model.  Cumulative pseudo-regret measures the expected reward lost, over all
rounds, by choosing the learner's action instead of the best action for each
context.  Lower values are better.  In this study, the Hessian policy
has higher mean pseudo-regret than every comparator in every condition.
Its regret bounds, and those of the frozen reference policy, are larger than
a simple deterministic cap.  The recorded confidence and optimism checks
nevertheless pass.  The findings separate those numerical checks from the
practical usefulness of the resulting scores and bounds.

The prespecified follow-up evaluates 600 policy trajectories; a separate 294
development and tuning trajectories precede evaluation.  We fix
$T=1000$ and $W\in\{16,1,0.25\}$, with 50 common evaluation seeds per condition
and four policies.  The model parameter $W$ changes both the reward function
and its curvature, so comparisons across conditions do not isolate an effect
of metric drift.  The original near-linear study appears separately in
\Cref{sec:transport-instantiation}; its evidence is not pooled with this study.

\paragraph{What differs between the policies?}
All policies use the corrected center and confidence radius from
\Cref{cor:corrected-center-new}.  They differ in how they turn a width into an
exploration bonus.  \emph{Hessian} inflates the current width using the analytic
path certificate $\bar D_t^Q$.  \emph{Endpoint} replaces that path certificate
with the dense endpoint Thompson distance.  \emph{Frozen reference} uses the
reference width directly.  \emph{Naive current} uses the current width without
transport and serves as an uncertified control.  All four policies update
their representations: ``frozen'' describes the metric's collection-time
gradients, not a fixed representation.  The policies receive matched contexts
and potential-noise streams, but their actions and subsequent learning
histories can differ.

\paragraph{Which policies incur less regret?}
\Cref{fig:transport-followup-outcomes} shows the higher mean regret of Hessian
transport in each condition; \Cref{tab:transport-followup-outcomes} gives the
numerical values.  The paired comparisons ask how much a comparator's regret
differs from Hessian's on the same evaluation seed.  All nine paired 95\%
intervals for this difference exclude zero in the comparator's favor.
These are pointwise intervals from 10,000 paired seed-bootstrap resamples per
comparison, without multiplicity adjustment.  They describe evaluation-seed
variation for the fixed teacher, design, and selected optimizer; they do not
include uncertainty from choosing the environment or tuning the optimizer.
Because policies follow their own adaptive histories, this comparison
measures the outcome of each complete policy.  It does not isolate the
effect of changing only a certificate at a fixed state or establish a
universal policy ordering.

For descriptive context, the online context-free mean-only benchmark has mean
regrets 185.26, 183.06, and 176.67 at $W=16,1,0.25$, respectively; the best fixed
action in hindsight has mean regrets 180.01, 177.97, and 171.84.  These means
use the same evaluation streams.  Hessian's sample means are close to the
context-free benchmark's, but no equivalence test was performed.  Similar
regret does not imply matching actions or identical use of context, and these
benchmarks do not bound the benefit available to another context-dependent
policy.  \Cref{app:transport-followup} defines both benchmarks.

\paragraph{Do the regret bounds give useful information?}
We judge these regret bounds by whether they improve on what the reward
range already guarantees.  That range gives the deterministic regret cap
$2T\min(1,\sqrt W)$, which is 2000, 2000, and 1000 for the three conditions.
We call a larger bound \emph{vacuous}: it adds no information beyond the cap.
Every Hessian-policy bound is vacuous.  The frozen policy's own
reference-geometry bounds also exceed the caps in all 150 of its trajectories.
\Cref{fig:transport-followup-bound-scale} shows the size of their mean
right-hand sides relative to the cap.  The frozen policy's mean bounds are
much smaller than Hessian's, but neither provides a useful regret upper bound
in this study.

The largest contribution to the Hessian bounds is path inflation, the extra
charge introduced by the path certificate, in all three conditions.  This
explains their scale within the bound calculation, but
removing that term from Hessian's histories would not give the bound or
outcome of the frozen policy: the latter follows different histories.
The original near-linear study instead reported base-term dominance; that
description does not apply to this follow-up.

\paragraph{What did the confidence and drift checks show?}
The unfavorable policy outcomes did not coincide with a recorded failure of
the confidence or optimism checks.  The former check whether the reference
bands contain the true mean rewards; the latter check whether the action
scores upper-bound those means.  All 600 evaluation trajectories,
including naive current, pass the simultaneous reference-confidence,
method-optimism, and deterministic diagnostics.  Each policy--condition cell
has 50 successes out of 50, with an exact 95\% Clopper--Pearson interval of
approximately $[92.9\%,100\%]$.  These are observed checks, not proof of
population coverage or of the need for transport.  Float64 checks and
converged quadrature are numerical diagnostics; they do not verify the
theorem's assumptions through rigorous numerical enclosures.

There is measurable movement between the reference and current matrices.
The primary policy reaches endpoint distances above one in 16 of 50 seeds at
$W=0.25$, including 286 of 50,000 seed--round observations
(\Cref{tab:transport-followup-drift-bounds}).  These round counts describe
dependent observations, not additional independent replications.  The target
labels used to encode $W$ are not measured endpoint distances.

\paragraph{Model and implementation details.}
The observations are Gaussian with mean
$\mu_{\btheta}(z)=\sqrt W\tanh(\phi(z)^\top\btheta/\sqrt W)$,
with $\|\phi(z)\|_2\le1$ and projected parameters $\|\btheta\|_2\le1$.
Contexts have dimension four, there are five actions, and the context--action
feature map has dimension 29.  Noise has standard deviation $0.25$, the metric
ridge is one, and the confidence parameter is $\delta=0.05$.
A fixed teacher generates the rewards.  The conditions were specified before
their evaluation outcomes were observed.  Development, tuning, and evaluation
use disjoint seed sets.

The shared optimizer uses learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$ with 20 full-batch
steps followed by projection each round.  It was selected from nine
candidates by prediction MSE on uniform-random tuning histories.  This fixes
one implementation choice across policies; it does not establish
policy-specific regret-optimal tuning.  Dense float64 matrices and Cholesky
solves implement the exact-operator abstraction.  Actions are enumerated,
with ties resolved by the smallest action index.  This 29-parameter study
therefore examines dense numerical implementations.  It does not test
approximate solvers, large-model scalability, or real-data performance.
\Cref{app:transport-followup} records the protocol and provenance.

\paragraph{What do the recorded decisions reveal?}
After observing the policy outcomes, we examined how the Hessian score's
width bonus related to its recommendations.  We independently recomputed score
diagnostics from all 600 preserved trajectories.  On Hessian's own histories,
its selected action equals the current-width argmax on 99.382\%, 99.962\%, and 99.982\% of
rounds for $W=16,1,0.25$.  A stronger sufficient condition---the bonus gap
between the two largest widths exceeding the entire range of prediction
centers---holds on 97.104\%, 99.372\%, and 99.508\% of rounds.
Under this condition, differences in predicted reward cannot overturn the
largest-width action's bonus advantage.  Argmax agreement means that the
policy chooses the action ranked first by current width under the same tie
rule.  The stronger condition identifies states where the bonus gap alone
forces that choice.  The rounds are dependent, and this retrospective
calculation does not identify an adaptive causal mechanism.

The per-seed table in \Cref{app:transport-followup} evaluates this same Hessian
score on histories generated by all four policies.  Its non-Hessian rows
describe what Hessian would recommend on those states, not how the other
policies' own scores choose actions.  The appendix also compares alternative
recommendations while holding a recorded history fixed and checks sensitivity
to early numerical rankings.  Changing a recommendation in such a calculation
does not reveal the later observations or decisions of a policy that actually
took that action.

\paragraph{How tight are the endpoint certificates on recorded states?}
The relative-weight construction in \Cref{cor:tanh-relative-weights} permits
uniform and category-specific endpoint comparisons without using the Hessian
path envelope.  After observing the outcomes, we audit these comparisons on
the same 600 recorded histories, replaying representation updates with the
recorded actions and rewards.  On primary histories, the category-specific
outward factor gives mean
effective-width overheads of $0.28\%$--$12.68\%$ relative to the direct reference
width, versus $0.16\%$--$12.13\%$ for the dense endpoint factor across conditions
(\Cref{app:transport-followup}).  These are means of actionwise ratios, not
ratios of mean widths: each effective width is compared with its reference
width before averaging.

This calculation shows the size of the certificate overhead on existing
states.  It evaluates neither a new adaptive policy nor end-to-end speed.
The relative certificate bounds the endpoint comparison, not the length of
the original interpolated factor path, and leaves the historical and current
approximation-error charges in place.  As \Cref{prop:reference-width-floor}
explains, tightening the certificate creates no additional statistical
information and does not order policies' adaptive regret.
